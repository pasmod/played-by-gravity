What you can do

Drop the balls and watch, or take over: drag to look around, pinch or scroll to come closer, and tap any tile, or a note name at the bottom, to play it yourself. You can slow the fall down without the notes going out of tune, and jump to any place in the song. On a phone you can add this page to your home screen; it then opens like an app and also works without a connection.

What you are watching

Two balls are let go at the top of a long fall. Every tile they land on is a note. Where a tile stands from left to right is its pitch, and how hard the ball lands on it is how loud the note sounds.

The balls are never moved by hand. A physics simulation with ordinary gravity, 9.81 m/s², carries them from the first tile to the last, and a tile can only take speed away from a ball, never add any. Each note you hear is triggered at the moment of its collision.

The song

“Sari Galin” is one of the best-loved old songs of Iran, the Caucasus and eastern Anatolia. It is sung in Azerbaijani, Armenian, Turkish and Persian, each with its own words; in Persian it is also known as “Dāman-keshān”. Nobody knows who wrote it. It is a song of longing for a beloved who cannot be reached.

You hear its melody twice: first plainly, then an octave higher. The notes are those of the melody as it was written down in a file on Wikimedia Commons; every one of them is played, in its order and its rhythm. The song is in three-four time, and its home note is D.

A struck string cannot be held, so the long notes are played as a tremolo, called riz: the two balls take turns on the same pitch, as the two hammers of a santur player do. The last notes of a phrase broaden a little, and a short breath separates the phrases.

How it was built

The music was written first, as a list of notes and times. A program then worked out, note by note, where each tile has to stand, how far it tilts and how bouncy it is, so that a falling ball arrives at the right place at the right moment. Bouncing magnifies the smallest error, so every tile was placed from the state of the running simulation and not from a formula.

When all the tiles stood, a second, separate run of the simulation had to play the whole piece again on its own. It had to strike every note of the score to within a millionth of a second, and no ball was allowed to brush any other tile on its way.

Loud is steep

A ball that falls further lands faster, and a faster landing is a louder note. So the loudness of the piece is written into the shape of the fall. Where the music grows, the staircase gets steeper, and an accent is a deeper drop into one note. A pause costs height too, because a ball that stays in the air longer comes down harder. That is also why the two balls share the work as they do: the ball that has waited longest lands hardest, so it is given the notes on the beat, and the quick notes in between go to the ball that has just landed.

The instrument

The santur is the Persian hammered dulcimer; the two balls are its two mezrabs, the light wooden hammers. Its sound is not a recording but a computed model of a course of four strings.
