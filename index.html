What you can do

Drop the balls and watch, or take over: drag to look around, pinch or scroll to come closer, and tap any tile, or a note name at the bottom, to play it yourself. You can slow the fall down without the notes going out of tune, jump between the four parts, and change the instrument while it plays. On a phone you can add this page to your home screen; it then opens like an app and also works without a connection.

What you are watching

Two balls are let go at the top of a long fall. Every tile they land on is a note. Where a tile stands from left to right is its pitch, and how hard the ball lands on it is how loud the note sounds.

The balls are never moved by hand. A physics simulation with ordinary gravity, 9.81 m/s², carries them from the first tile to the last, and a tile can only take speed away from a ball, never add any. Each note you hear is triggered at the moment of its collision.

The piece

“Shur in g” is an original piece in Dastgāh-e Shur, one of the seven modal systems of Persian classical music, here built on the note G. It has four parts. The Darāmad, the opening, is free in rhythm and circles the home note. The Chahārmezrāb is fast, in 6/8, over a G that keeps returning. The Owj, the peak, climbs into the high octave. The Forud brings everything back down to G.

Shur uses two notes a piano does not have: A koron and D koron, each about a quarter tone lower than A and D. Their tiles stand exactly where the pitch puts them, between their neighbours.

A struck or plucked string cannot be held, so long notes are played as a tremolo, called riz: the two balls take turns on the same pitch. Where a phrase ends, the balls land on soft bars, bounce a few times and lie still. That is the silence between phrases. Then they roll off the end and fall into the next one.

This is a gusheh, one of the short traditional pieces of the radif, the repertoire through which Persian classical music is taught and handed down. It belongs to Bayāt-e Esfahān. Its home note here is G, the F sharp just below leans into it, and its E is a koron, about a quarter tone low. That mixture gives the mode its bittersweet colour.

The notes are those of the radif of Mirzā Abdollāh (1843–1918), as written down by Dariush Talai and entered note by note into the Radif Corpus. Every note of that transcription is played here, in its order and its proportion. The longest notes are played as a tremolo, called riz, the way a setar or santur holds a note, and a short breath separates the phrases.

The radif has no beat. Its notes have fixed proportions, but how fast they go is the player's choice; here it is ours.

How it was built

The music was written first, as a list of notes and times. A program then worked out, note by note, where each tile has to stand, how far it tilts and how bouncy it is, so that a falling ball arrives at the right place at the right moment. Bouncing magnifies the smallest error, so every tile was placed from the state of the running simulation and not from a formula.

When all the tiles stood, a second, separate run of the simulation had to play the whole piece again on its own. It had to strike every note of the score to within a millionth of a second, and no ball was allowed to brush any other tile on its way.

Loud is steep

A ball that falls further lands faster, and a faster landing is a louder note. So the loudness of the piece is written into the shape of the fall. Where the music grows, the staircase gets steeper, and an accent is a deeper drop into one note. A pause costs height too, because a ball that stays in the air longer comes down harder.

The two instruments

The setar is a small long-necked lute, plucked with the nail of the index finger. Its notes here are real: single plucks of a setar, recorded fret by fret, with the instrument's own koron frets. When the next note is played on a string, the one before it stops, as under a player's finger, and a strong stroke also brushes the open drone strings.

The santur is the Persian hammered dulcimer; there the two balls are its two mezrabs, the light wooden hammers. Its sound is not a recording but a computed model of a course of four strings, and every note rings on over the next.
