What you can do

Press play and watch; the camera finds its own way. Tap any tile, or a note name at the bottom, to play it yourself. You can slow the fall down without the notes going out of tune, and jump to any place in the piece. On a phone you can add this page to your home screen; it then opens like an app and also works without a connection.

What you are watching

Two balls are let go at the top of a long fall. Every tile they land on is a note. Where a tile stands from left to right is its pitch, and how hard the ball lands on it is how loud the note sounds. Only a low drone string far below the melody stands closer than its pitch would put it, so that a ball can reach it in time.

The balls are never moved by hand. A physics simulation with ordinary gravity, 9.81 m/s², carries them from the first tile to the last, and a tile can only take speed away from a ball, never add any. Each note you hear is triggered at the moment of its collision.

The piece

It is a gusheh, one of the short traditional pieces of the radif, the repertoire through which Persian classical music is taught and handed down. The notes are those of the radif of Mirzā Abdollāh (1843–1918), as written down by Dariush Talai and entered note by note into the Radif Corpus. Every note of that transcription is played here, in its order and its length; only the speed is our choice. The koron notes, about a quarter tone low and not found on a piano, have tiles of their own between their neighbours.

A struck string cannot be held, so the longest notes are played as a tremolo, called riz: the two balls take turns on the same pitch, as the two hammers of a santur player do.

How it was built

The music was written first, as a list of notes and times. A program then worked out, note by note, where each tile has to stand, how far it tilts and how bouncy it is, so that a falling ball arrives at the right place at the right moment. Bouncing magnifies the smallest error, so every tile was placed from the state of the running simulation and not from a formula.

When all the tiles stood, a second, separate run of the simulation had to play the whole piece again on its own. It had to strike every note of the score to within a millionth of a second, and no ball was allowed to brush any other tile on its way.

Loud is steep

A ball that falls further lands faster, and a faster landing is a louder note. So the loudness of the piece is written into the shape of the fall. Where the music grows, the staircase gets steeper, and an accent is a deeper drop into one note. A pause costs height too, because a ball that stays in the air longer comes down harder. That is also why the two balls share the work as they do: the ball that has waited longest lands hardest, so it is given the long notes, and the quick notes in between go to the ball that has just landed.

The instrument

The santur is the Persian hammered dulcimer; the two balls are its two mezrabs, the light wooden hammers. Its sound is not a recording but a computed model of a course of four strings.
