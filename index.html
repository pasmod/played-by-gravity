What you are watching

Two balls are let go at the top of a long fall. Every tile they land on is a note. Where a tile stands from left to right is its pitch, and how hard the ball lands on it is how loud the note sounds.

That is all there is to it. The balls are never moved by hand. A physics simulation with ordinary gravity, 9.81 m/s², carries them from the first tile to the last, and a tile can only take speed away from a ball, never add any.

One ball is gold and one is silver, so you can follow each of them. You can pause, move through the piece on the line at the bottom, and drag the picture to look around.

The same collisions can be heard on two instruments; you choose at the bottom right. The setar is a small long-necked lute, plucked with the nail of the index finger: here the two balls are the two directions of the nail, down and up. The santur is the Persian hammered dulcimer: there the balls are its two mezrabs, the light wooden hammers.

The piece

“Shur in g” is an original piece in Dastgāh-e Shur, one of the seven modal systems of Persian classical music, here built on the note G. It has four parts. The Darāmad, the opening, is free in rhythm and circles the home note. The Chahārmezrāb is fast, in 6/8, over a G that keeps returning. The Owj, the peak, climbs into the high octave. The Forud brings everything back down to G.

Shur uses two notes a piano does not have: A koron and D koron, each about a quarter tone lower than A and D. Their tiles stand exactly where the pitch puts them, between their neighbours.

A struck string cannot be held, so long notes are played as a tremolo, called riz: the two balls take turns on the same pitch. Where a phrase ends, the balls land on soft bars, bounce a few times and lie still. That is the silence between phrases. Then they roll off the end and fall into the next one.

How it was built

The music was written first, as a list of notes and times. A program then worked out, note by note, where each tile has to stand, how far it tilts and how bouncy it is, so that a falling ball arrives at the right place at the right moment. Bouncing magnifies the smallest error, so every tile was placed from the state of the running simulation and not from a formula.

When all the tiles stood, a second, separate run of the simulation had to play the whole piece again on its own. It had to strike every note of the score to within a millionth of a second, and no ball was allowed to brush any other tile on its way.

Loud is steep

A ball that falls further lands faster, and a faster landing is a louder note. So the loudness of the piece is written into the shape of the fall. Where the music grows, the staircase gets steeper, and an accent is a deeper drop into one note.

A pause costs height too, because a ball that stays in the air longer comes down harder. That is why the slow, spacious notes ring out and the fast tremolo strokes, which are only small hops, stay soft.

The sound

The sound is not a recording of an instrument. Each collision in the simulation sets off a computed model of a string. How hard the ball hit decides how loud and how bright the note is.

On the setar it is one plucked steel string in a small wooden body. When the next note is played, the one before it stops, as it does under a player's finger, and a strong stroke also brushes the open drone strings. On the santur it is a course of four strings tuned a hair apart, struck by a hammer, and every note rings on over the next.
